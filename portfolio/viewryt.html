Context

What is ViewRyt?

ViewRyt is a macular health supplement by The Ayurveda Experience, a direct-to-consumer wellness brand with 1.5 million customers across 150+ countries. The product contains the full AREDS 2 formula — the NIH-sponsored clinical standard for reducing advanced age-related macular degeneration (AMD) risk by 25%.

The brand runs paid ads on Meta driving traffic to Shopify landing pages. Each page variant is named by an internal convention: NLP7 was the page built by a previous designer. I was briefed to create NLP8 — a redesign with identical copy for better conversion performance.

This constraint — same copy, different design — created a near-perfect natural experiment. The only variable was design. Everything else: the product, the price, the claims, the ad creative, the audience targeting, remained constant.

The Problem

What was wrong with NLP7?

NLP7 wasn't broken. It had decent structure, the right content, and a working page flow. But with a CVR of 1.44% and ROAS of 0.32, the ad algorithm couldn't scale it — it was losing money on every dollar spent. A profitable page earns more budget. An unprofitable page dies.

My audit identified four structural problems:

  • 01

    Trust signals buried below the fold

    The hero jumped straight to symptom identification — a fear-forward hook with no credibility context. Institution references (Johns Hopkins, NIH, Yale) and expert endorsements appeared 40–60% down the page, long after most users had made their trust decision.

  • 02

    Cognitive overload before the first CTA

    The page front-loaded mechanism explanation — three disease pathways, six nutrient breakdowns, molecular diagrams — before the reader had any emotional reason to care. High cognitive load + low trust = exit.

  • 03

    CTA was visually undifferentiated

    Purchase buttons blended into the surrounding content. No contrast hierarchy between primary CTAs (buy now) and secondary actions (learn more). The decision point was unclear.

  • 04

    Social proof arrived too late

    Customer testimonials and the competitive comparison table appeared in the bottom third of the page. Readers who hadn't converted by that point were unlikely to reach them. The most persuasive content was in the lowest-traffic zone.

Design Approach

The conversion hypothesis

Long-form health product pages follow a trust arc: the reader moves from skepticism → curiosity → belief → desire → decision. NLP7's structure assumed the reader would patiently follow the page top-to-bottom. Real readers don't.

My hypothesis: if I could compress the journey from skepticism to belief into the first scroll, the rest of the page would do its job. I restructured around four design principles:

Trust First

Authority signals above the fold. The reader's first question is always "can I trust this?" — answer it before they even start reading.

Earn Attention Before Explaining

Social proof and outcomes before mechanism. Give the reader a reason to care about the science before presenting it.

Decision Architecture

Clear visual hierarchy between the primary CTA and everything else. The decision point should feel inevitable, not like work.

Progressive Disclosure

Surface enough to satisfy curiosity at each scroll depth. Skeptics get trust signals. Curious readers get mechanism. Believers get to buy.

Key Design Decisions

Five changes that moved the needle

Each decision below is grounded in conversion principles and observable user behavior patterns for long-form health product pages.

01

Hero Section — Authority Before Fear

The original opened with symptoms. I opened with credibility.

NLP7 — Before

Large fear-forward headline ("5 Silent Signs of Early AMD") immediately followed by risk statistics. Institutional credentials appeared below a full-page illustration, far from the hero. First-time visitors had no anchor for trusting the claim.

NLP8 — After

Restructured hero to pair the headline with inline credibility markers (Johns Hopkins, NIH, Yale) rendered as trust badges, visible without scrolling. The fear headline is still present — it works — but it's now backstopped by institutional authority within the same viewport.

Why it matters

For health products, trust is the conversion variable — not desire. The reader already wants better vision. The question is whether they trust the product enough to buy. Front-loading credibility compresses the skepticism-to-belief arc.

02

Social Proof — Move It Up, Not Down

Testimonials should greet skeptics, not reward survivors.

NLP7 — Before

Customer reviews ("Real Eyes, Real Results") appeared in the bottom third of the page — after the full scientific explanation, ingredient deep-dive, and competitor comparison. Most users never reached this section.

NLP8 — After

Moved a condensed testimonial strip to appear immediately after the problem section — before the mechanism explanation. Readers encounter real customer outcomes before they're asked to engage with science. The full review section remains intact lower on the page.

Why it matters

Social proof reduces perceived risk. Placing it early means the reader evaluates the scientific content through the frame of "other people found this credible" rather than approaching it cold and skeptical.

03

CTA Design — Make the Decision Feel Inevitable

A button that looks like everything else converts like nothing.

NLP7 — Before

Purchase buttons used standard Shopify styling with minimal visual differentiation from surrounding UI. Primary and secondary actions had similar visual weight. The "add to cart" moment had no visual ceremony.

NLP8 — After

Redesigned the primary CTA with high contrast, full-width treatment on mobile, surrounded by micro-trust signals (guarantee badge, payment icons, security copy) immediately adjacent. The CTA zone is designed as a distinct visual moment — a stage for the decision.

Why it matters

The CTA is not just a button — it's a zone. Everything around the button is part of the conversion event. Pairing high visual contrast with risk-reduction signals (guarantee, security) addresses the two remaining objections at the exact moment of decision.

04

Comparison Table — Designed for Scanning, Not Reading

Tables that require effort don't get read. Tables that reward scanning do.

NLP7 — Before

The competitor comparison table used dense, equal-weight typography with no visual hierarchy between row labels, values, and advantage indicators. On mobile, horizontal scrolling made it effectively invisible.

NLP8 — After

Redesigned as a stacked card comparison on mobile with clear checkmark/cross visual language. Each advantage is immediately scannable without reading. The "ViewRyt" column uses higher visual weight, making the win state obvious at a glance.

Why it matters

Comparison tables are used to justify a decision already emotionally made. They need to confirm, not convince. If scanning the table takes effort, readers skip it — losing the reinforcement at a critical moment.

05

Ingredient Visualization — Science That Earns, Not Intimidates

The AREDS 2 formula is the product's strongest argument. Most visitors never understood it.

NLP7 — Before

Six ingredients were presented as individual text blocks with dense mechanism explanations. The visual treatment gave equal weight to all six, with no sense of hierarchy or cumulative effect. It read like a supplement label, not a compelling argument.

NLP8 — After

Redesigned the ingredient section with a lead card for the complete AREDS 2 formula (the clinical whole), followed by individual ingredient cards. The structure communicates: "here's the gold standard formula — here's what each part does." Visual weight matches claim weight.

Why it matters

The AREDS 2 study is the product's most credible claim — NIH-sponsored, 4,203 participants, 10 years. This needed to be a headline moment, not buried in a list. Restructuring around "formula first, ingredients second" made the clinical backing land with the weight it deserves.

Results

The numbers, in full

The data below is from ClickHouse (Meta ad platform) and Klaviyo, covering the US market from May 2025 to May 2026. NLP7 and NLP8 were served to the same audience with comparable ad creative.

Version Spend (USD) Revenue (USD) ROAS CVR EPV
NLP7 — Previous designer $309 $100 0.32 1.44% $0.36
NLP8 — Ravindra (redesign) $9,798 $10,398 1.06 4.65% $1.50
Improvement — — +231% +222% +314%

NLP8 received 31× more ad spend because it earned it. When ROAS crossed 1.0, the algorithm identified the page as profitable and scaled budget automatically. NLP7 was paused; NLP8 became the primary US landing page.

The Budget Multiplier Effect

A conversion rate improvement doesn't scale linearly — it compounds. A page that converts at 4.65% doesn't just make more money per visitor; it earns a larger audience from the algorithm, which means more visitors, which means more revenue, which means more budget. NLP8 didn't just outperform NLP7. It replaced it.

Reflection

What I'd do differently

The performance data is compelling, but it's a sequential comparison — NLP7 ran, then NLP8 ran — not a simultaneous A/B split. That said, the same-copy constraint is about as clean a natural experiment as you'll get in production. The only variable was design.

If I were running this again: I'd push for A/B test infrastructure from the start. A true split would definitively isolate design as the variable and make the causal claim watertight for stakeholders. I'd also instrument scroll depth and heatmaps from day one — the quantitative lift is clear, but understanding which decisions drove it requires behavioral data I didn't have.

What I'd test next: A video testimonial above the fold (hypothesis: addresses skepticism faster than text), a shorter mobile variant (hypothesis: reduced scroll depth = less drop-off), and a price anchor section earlier in the page (hypothesis: establishing value before presenting price reduces sticker shock).

The bigger lesson: this project proved that design has measurable ROI — not in the abstract, but in dollars and percentage points. That's the argument I now make in every brief.
