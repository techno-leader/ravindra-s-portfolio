IRBS

Redesigning IIT Guwahati's room reservation system — from lost QR codes and verbal workarounds to a unified booking platform used daily by 200+ students.

Context

What is IRBS?

IRBS (Institute Room Booking System) is the room reservation infrastructure for IIT Guwahati — one of India's premier technical institutes. The campus hosts dozens of student clubs running events, meetings, and activities that require booking shared spaces.

Four categories of bookable spaces exist on campus, each with different use cases, capacity requirements, and access rules:

Club Rooms

Small meetings, equipment storage, and day-to-day club operations. High-frequency bookings, short durations.

Common Rooms

Large gatherings, yoga sessions, and campus-wide events. Shared access, longer durations, higher conflict potential.

Board Rooms

Administrative use and formal meetings. Access controlled, fewer bookings but higher stakes.

Unallocated Rooms

Spaces without permanent assignment — available on a first-come basis but difficult to discover through the old system.

The Problem

Why the old system was failing

A booking portal existed. The problem wasn't absence — it was abandonment. Students defaulted to verbal confirmation and word-of-mouth because the existing system was too broken to trust. Three structural failures drove this:

  • 01

    Discoverability — the system was invisible

    The portal relied on a QR code pinned to room doors. QR codes get lost, replaced, or simply ignored. Students had no reliable path to the booking interface — especially new club members who hadn't been briefed by seniors. The system existed outside the daily campus digital ecosystem, making it friction-heavy to reach.

  • 02

    Functionality — broken flows and missing guardrails

    The interface had no booking duration limits, so a conference hall could be (and was) reserved for an entire week by a single club. There was no rejection workflow — admins couldn't decline a request with a reason. Incomplete form submissions created ghost bookings. The interface confused users into booking the wrong space for the wrong time.

  • 03

    Scale — festival periods created unresolvable conflicts

    During college festivals, demand for shared spaces spikes dramatically. Multiple clubs compete for the same rooms with no hierarchical control, no priority framework, and no conflict resolution mechanism. Senior clubs and festival organizers had no way to pre-allocate or protect spaces critical to the event.

"We just ask the sir directly. The system doesn't work properly — last time someone booked the hall for five days and we couldn't get it for our event."

— Club Secretary, IIT Guwahati (Research Interview)

Research

Listening before designing

The core research question wasn't "how do we improve the UI?" It was: "Why do students bypass the system entirely, even when it exists?" Understanding the workarounds was more important than cataloguing the bugs.

I interviewed three distinct stakeholder groups across 11 sessions, probing for awareness, experience, usage patterns, and the specific moments where the system broke down:

2 Club VPs
(decision makers)
4 Secretaries
(power users)
5 General students
(typical users)

Three themes crystallized from the interviews:

Discoverability gap

OneStop — the campus super-app for food, travel, and scheduling — had daily active usage. The booking portal had none. Students knew OneStop by habit; the room system only by QR code.

Functional distrust

Past failures had poisoned trust. Students had tried the system, experienced bugs or conflicts, and reverted to verbal workarounds. The system's reputation was worse than its current reality.

Scale asymmetry

Individual bookings were manageable. Festival-scale coordination — 30+ clubs competing for 8 rooms over 4 days — required hierarchical controls the system didn't have.

Admin opacity

Secretaries managing bookings had no visibility into pending requests, no way to communicate decisions to requesters, and no audit trail. Every approval happened off-system.

Key Design Decisions

Four changes that rebuilt trust in the system

Each decision addressed a specific failure mode uncovered in research. The guiding constraint: integrate into OneStop, not build another standalone portal.

01

Embed into OneStop — meet users where they already are

A better portal nobody visits is still a portal nobody visits.

Before — Standalone Portal

The legacy system was a separate web app, accessible only via a QR code on room doors. No persistent URL, no home in any existing campus app. Students had to know about it — and remember how to reach it — every single time.

After — OneStop Integration

IRBS was embedded directly into OneStop — the same app students use daily for food orders, transport, and timetables. Room booking became a tab, not a detour. Discoverability went from zero to guaranteed.

Why it matters

The biggest adoption barrier wasn't the UI — it was the activation cost. Students wouldn't open a new app for an occasional task. But they'd tap a new tab in an app they already had open. Embedding in OneStop eliminated the cold-start problem entirely.

02

Three-step booking flow — structured simplicity

A form with no clear end-state generates incomplete submissions. A flow with clear steps doesn't.

Before — Monolithic Form

The old interface presented all booking fields on a single screen with no step indicators, no validation feedback, and no confirmation state. Users submitted incomplete forms without knowing it. Ghost bookings accumulated with no one accountable.

After — Progressive Steps

Restructured as a three-step flow: (1) select room and verify availability, (2) pick time slot via calendar interface, (3) state purpose and submit. Each step validates before advancing. A confirmation screen closes the loop.

Why it matters

Progress indicators reduce abandonment by making completion feel achievable. Validation at each step catches errors early, when they're cheap to fix — not at submission, when they're frustrating. The confirmation screen creates a psychological contract: your request is real and tracked.

01

Room Selection & Availability

Browse rooms by type, see real-time availability, and select the right space before committing to a time.

02

Time Slot via Calendar

Visual calendar interface showing occupied and available slots. Duration guardrails prevent week-long blocks.

03

Purpose Statement & Submit

State the booking purpose to help admins triage requests. Submission triggers a secretary notification.

03

Admin workflow — close the loop on every request

An approval that happens off-system doesn't fix the system's trust problem.

Before — Off-System Decisions

Secretaries received no notification when requests came in. Approvals happened via WhatsApp messages or in-person. Students had no way to know if their request was seen, pending, or rejected. The booking status was always uncertain.

After — Closed-Loop Admin Panel

Secretaries receive in-app notifications on submission. They can accept or reject with a stated reason. Frequent users can be granted pre-approval permissions. Every decision is logged and visible to the requester — no ambiguity.

Why it matters

Trust in a booking system comes from predictability — knowing that a request will be actioned, and knowing why if it isn't. The previous system had no answer for "what happened to my request?" The new admin workflow makes every outcome visible, which is what converts occasional users into habitual ones.

04

Permission tiers — designed for scale, not just typical use

A system that works for 10 bookings a week but fails during festival week isn't reliable.

Before — Flat Access

All users had the same access level. No priority for senior clubs, no pre-allocation for major events, no way to reserve spaces for festival-critical use. During high-demand periods, the system devolved into first-come chaos.

After — Tiered Permissions

Introduced a permission framework: frequent users earn pre-approval status, reducing admin load for routine requests. Festival organizers get temporary elevated access for event periods. Equipment usage guidelines surface in-context, reducing misuse.

Why it matters

Campus infrastructure tools fail in predictable ways at peak load. Building permission tiers into the initial design meant the system didn't need to be rebuilt when festival season arrived — it scaled to the use case rather than collapsing under it.

Beta Testing & Iterations

What changed during testing

Beta testing with club secretaries and students surfaced friction points that weren't visible in design reviews. Three significant changes came directly from observed behavior — not assumptions:

  • Removed empty-state messaging for unused booking slots. Early designs showed "No bookings yet" placeholders in the calendar view. Testing showed users interpreted this as an error rather than an available state — they'd abandon the booking attempt thinking something was broken.

  • Deferred drag-select calendar to a future release. The original spec included a drag-to-select time range in the calendar. Beta users found it unreliable on touch devices and inconsistent with the rest of the OneStop interaction pattern. We shipped a simpler tap-to-select flow and logged drag-select as a post-launch improvement.

  • Fixed back-navigation to return to previous screen, not home. An early build redirected users to the OneStop home screen after completing a booking. Testing revealed this broke the mental model — users expected to land back on the room list to make additional bookings, not be ejected to an unrelated context.

Impact

From workaround to workflow

IRBS launched in September 2023 as part of OneStop. Within the first semester, adoption exceeded expectations — not because of a campaign, but because the system was finally trustworthy enough to use.

The Adoption Signal

When a formerly bypassed tool becomes the default workflow without any mandated rollout, that's the signal that the design worked. Clubs stopped asking sirs directly. Secretaries stopped managing requests over WhatsApp. The system became the source of truth because it earned that position.

15+ clubs actively using the system

Across club types, room categories, and booking frequencies — from daily small-group use to festival-scale multi-day allocations.

200+ daily student users

Consistent daily active usage in the first semester after launch, embedded naturally into existing OneStop habits.

Zero verbal workarounds reported

Follow-up interviews with club VPs and secretaries confirmed that off-system booking (WhatsApp messages, in-person requests) had effectively stopped.

Simplified meeting coordination

Secretaries cited the closed-loop notification system as the single biggest time saver — decisions made in-app replaced hours of follow-up messages.

Reflection

What I'd do differently

The biggest design decision on this project wasn't a UI choice — it was the integration strategy. Embedding IRBS into OneStop rather than improving the standalone portal solved a problem that better UI alone never could have. That insight came from research, not assumption.

What I'd instrument from day one: I had no analytics on the old system and limited behavioral data from the new one post-launch. Tracking drop-off rates by booking step, time-to-completion, and admin response times would have given me quantitative evidence to match the qualitative signal. Designing without measurement is designing half-blind.

What I'd test next: A proactive room suggestion feature — "Rooms often available at this time" — surfaced at the moment of search. Hypothesis: reducing the selection step from a choice to a recommendation would cut booking time by 40% for recurring use cases.

The broader lesson: trust is the metric that matters most for tools people don't have to use. IRBS existed for years in a form students actively avoided. The design didn't need to be clever — it needed to be reliable. That's a fundamentally different brief, and one worth understanding before picking up a pen.
